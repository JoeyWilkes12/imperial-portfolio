2022
Research · HPC · ML

Source-backed project report

Learning Using Thinned Networks: A Crowdsourcing Phenomena in Reservoir Computing

Question

The report studies whether aggressively thinning the internal networks of reservoir computers can improve how they reconstruct chaotic dynamics.

Approach

The team compared thinned and conventional reservoirs across more than 100 million numerical experiments, then connected performance to node independence, response diversity, and spectral radius.

Reported finding

Highly thinned networks often outperformed conventional networks. The paper frames the effect through a crowdsourcing analogy and proposes spectral-radius guidance.

Authored by J. Jamieson, D. J. Passey, B. Webb, and J. Wilkes.

2021
NLP · Classification

Source-backed project report

A Natural Language Processing Analysis of General Conference Discourses

Question

The project explored how a multi-decade corpus of public discourses could support recommendation, topic analysis, and classification tasks.

Approach

The report applies latent semantic indexing and non-negative matrix factorization for similarity, LDACGS and classifiers for topics, and Hidden Markov Models for topic and speaker classification.

Scope note

This record describes the archived team report without independently re-validating its results.

Authored by Clark Brown, Mikelle Rogers, Daniel Swingle, Joseph Wilkes, and Caleb Wilson.

2020
Simulation · Modeling

Source-backed project report

RISK Game Prediction

Question

Can the eventual winner of a simulated strategy game be predicted from a snapshot of the board before the game ends?

Approach

The team generated tens of thousands of simulated PyRisk games, engineered board-state features, and compared predictive models at different stages of play.

Reported finding

Accuracy rose as games progressed. The report explicitly cautions that the data represents AI players rather than humans.

Authored by Jacob Murri, Joseph Wilkes, Caleb Wilson, and Lukas Erekson.

2019
Regression · Analysis

Source-backed project report

Let's All Go to the Movies

Question

How well can publicly available movie features predict domestic and worldwide box-office revenue?

Approach

The team compared several machine-learning methods and reported separate model choices for domestic and worldwide revenue.

Reported finding

The report favors K-nearest neighbors for domestic revenue and backpropagation for worldwide revenue, while noting selection bias in a dataset concentrated on high-grossing films.

Authored by Joseph Wilkes, Jacob Rohde, and Pauline Caussé.
